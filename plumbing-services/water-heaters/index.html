Water Heater Services for DFW Homes

The water heater is the appliance everybody forgets until it quits. It sits in the garage or a closet for 10 years, then leaks, goes cold or starts banging, and you’ve got a morning to sort it out. North Texas makes it harder. Our water is hard, and the minerals settle at the bottom of a tank, insulate the burner or element and shorten the unit’s life.

So most of our calls fall into two buckets. Something’s broken, like a pilot that won’t stay lit, a bad element, a leaking valve or a tank that doesn’t keep up anymore. Or the unit’s old enough that you’re deciding between one more repair and a replacement. We’ll look at the age, the condition and what’s wrong, then tell you plainly which makes sense and what each costs.

If you do replace, you’ve got two paths. A conventional tank water heater is the familiar, lower-cost choice. A tankless water heater heats on demand and takes up a fraction of the space. We’ll help you pick based on your household, your gas or electric setup and your budget. Water heater work is also part of the wider plumbing services we provide across DFW.

Water Heater Repair, Replacement and Maintenance

The obvious one is no hot water. The quieter ones are easy to miss: hot water that runs out halfway through a shower, rust-colored water from the hot tap, popping or rumbling from the tank, or a puddle around the base. Scale is usually behind the noise. Sediment sits on the bottom, traps water under it and boils it into those pops. If your heater is 8 to 12 years old and doing two or more of these, start planning for it.

A lot of water heater problems are cheap to fix. On a gas unit, it’s often a worn thermocouple, a dirty burner or a pilot light that won’t stay lit. On an electric unit, it’s usually a failed heating element or a bad thermostat. Either type can also need a new relief valve, drain valve, dip tube or anode rod. We find the cause, show you what failed and fix it on the spot when we have the part. We repair both tank and tankless units, and the tankless side gets more detail on its own tankless water heater page.

One thing we can’t fix is a leaking tank. If the water’s coming from the tank itself, it’s rusted through and the unit has to go.

If your heater is leaking, shut off the cold water supply valve on top of the unit and switch off the power or gas to it. If you smell gas, leave the area immediately and, from a safe location, call 911 and your gas utility’s emergency number. For other water-heater issues, call (940) 370-5663 to discuss availability and the next steps.

Here’s how we usually call it.

If it’s a close call, we’ll give you both numbers and let you decide.

Replacement is more than swapping tanks. We size the new unit to your household. A 40 or 50 gallon tank handles most homes, and bigger families may need more or a tankless system. We check the venting or electrical supply, set up a drain pan, add an expansion tank where it’s needed and make sure the relief valve is piped out safely. Most DFW cities require a permit for a water heater swap, and we handle it. Then we haul away the old unit.

A conventional tank water heater costs less upfront and is simple to service. The tradeoff is that it stores and constantly reheats 40 to 50 gallons whether you use it or not, and it eventually runs out. A tankless heats only what you use, so the hot water doesn’t run out, and the unit takes up very little room. It costs more to install, and in hard-water areas it needs periodic descaling. See our pages on tank water heaters and tankless water heaters for the details.

Hard water is why maintenance matters here. A yearly flush clears sediment before it hardens. Every few years, the anode rod should be checked, since it’s the part that keeps the tank from rusting out. We also test the relief valve and make sure the thermostat is set to around 120 degrees, which is hot enough for comfort and low enough to avoid scalds and wasted energy. A tank that’s looked after often outlasts one that isn’t by several years.

How do I know when to replace my water heater?

Age is the first clue. A tank that’s 10 or more years old and showing rust, leaks or repeated problems is usually due. Water leaking from the tank itself means replacement, since a rusted tank can’t be patched. Younger units with a bad element, valve or pilot are usually worth repairing.

What’s the difference between tank and tankless water heaters?

A tank heater stores 40 to 50 gallons and keeps it hot. A tankless heater warms water only when you open a tap. Tank units cost less up front. Tankless units don’t run out of hot water and take up much less room, but they cost more to install and need descaling in hard water. The comparison section above covers it in more detail.

Do you handle gas and electric heaters?

Yes, both. We repair and install gas and electric units, tank and tankless.

Can I upgrade to a tankless water heater from a tank model?

Yes. It usually means a larger gas line and new venting, or more electrical capacity if yours is electric. We’ll check what your house needs before we quote, so there aren’t surprises on install day. Our tankless water heater page goes into the details.

How long do water heaters last?

A conventional tank typically lasts 8 to 12 years, and less in hard-water areas if it isn’t flushed. A tankless heater can last around 20 years with regular descaling.

Do you serve rural and city homes alike?

Yes. We work in town and on larger rural lots around Argyle. If your home runs on propane or well water, mention it when you call so we can come prepared.

Do you pull permits for water heater installs?

Yes, all water heater installations are completed in compliance with local building codes and safety regulations. We handle necessary permits and inspections where required, ensuring your installation meets all city and state standards.

Is financing available for water heater services?

Yes, we offer flexible financing options for water heater installation and replacement. Through our trusted local financing partners, homeowners can access affordable payment plans to upgrade or replace their systems without upfront financial strain.
